A Brief History: From Symbolic AI to Modern Machine Learning

The Early Rule-Based Era (1950s-1980s)

The first wave of AI, starting in the 1950s, was built on rules written by hand. Engineers and domain experts sat down and wrote out explicit instructions: "if the patient has symptom X and test result Y, then consider diagnosis Z." Systems built this way were called expert systems, because they tried to capture an expert's knowledge as a long list of if-then rules.

Expert systems could be genuinely useful inside their narrow area — a well-built medical or engineering expert system could match a specialist's advice on the exact situations its rules covered. The catch was fragility: the moment a real situation fell slightly outside the rules that had been written, the system had nothing sensible to say. It could not learn or adapt on its own; every new situation needed a programmer to add another rule.

The AI Winters: When Funding and Hype Collapsed

Researchers in the 1950s-60s made bold promises — some predicted machines that would match human intelligence within a couple of decades. When those promises didn't materialise on schedule, governments and investors pulled funding, and progress stalled for years at a time. These funding droughts are known as "AI winters" — the first through the 1970s, a second in the late 1980s and early 1990s, after a wave of expensive expert systems failed to live up to their promised returns.

Key Point. The AI winters happened because expectations ran far ahead of what the technology could actually deliver. It's a pattern worth remembering: when a client asks about a bold new AI claim, ask what it can concretely do today, not what it might do "soon."

Each winter ended once a new approach proved itself well enough to earn back trust and funding — which is exactly what happened next.

The Shift to Learning From Data

Instead of hand-writing every rule, researchers moved toward statistical machine learning: systems that are shown large amounts of past data and learn the patterns themselves, rather than being told the rules directly. A spam filter, for example, isn't given a fixed list of "spam words" — it learns from thousands of examples of spam and normal mail what tends to distinguish the two.

This shift, combined with vastly more data and far more computing power becoming available from the 2000s onward, set up the more recent deep-learning boom: large models trained on enormous datasets, powering the chatbots, image tools, and forecasting features that suddenly seem to be everywhere Brian looks. The ingredients — data, computing power, and better learning techniques — took decades to come together, even though the results feel new.
