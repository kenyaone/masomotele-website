What Artificial Intelligence Actually Is

So What Is AI, Really?

Strip away the hype and the definition is fairly simple: Artificial Intelligence (AI) is software built to perform tasks that would normally require a person's judgement — things like spotting a pattern in a pile of numbers, understanding a sentence written in ordinary language, or predicting what is likely to happen next based on past data.

That's it. There is no single "AI brain" sitting somewhere making decisions. Instead, there are many different AI tools, each built or trained to be good at one particular kind of judgement task. A spam filter is AI. A tool that predicts next month's sales from past sales is AI. A chat assistant that answers questions in plain English is AI. They are all software — just software built to do something that used to need a human to look at it first.

Narrow AI vs the Idea of General AI

Every AI tool Brian is likely to meet at Rafiki Insights is what's called narrow AI (sometimes "weak AI"): built for one job, and only that job. A spreadsheet forecasting tool is very good at predicting numbers, but it cannot hold a conversation. A spam filter is excellent at spotting junk email, but it cannot forecast sales. Each tool is a specialist, not an all-rounder.

General AI is a different, much bigger idea: a hypothetical system that could learn and reason across any task a human can, the way a person can move from writing a report to fixing a leaking tap to comforting a friend. General AI does not exist yet — every AI product on the market today, including the impressive ones, is narrow AI wearing a confident interface.

Key Point. If you remember one thing from this lesson, remember this: today's AI is narrow — a specialist tool for one kind of task — not an all-knowing general mind. Keep that distinction in your back pocket whenever a client asks what an "AI feature" can and can't do.

Clearing Up the Sci-Fi Picture

Films and news headlines often show AI as a self-aware robot with its own goals — deciding, plotting, wanting things. That picture makes for a good film, but it has very little to do with the software Rafiki Insights actually works with. A forecasting tool does not "want" the forecast to be right; it has no wants at all. It runs the calculations it was built to run, on the data it is given, and produces an output.

Recognising this gap between the sci-fi image and the reality matters for Brian's day job: it means AI tools should be judged the same way any other business software is judged — by what task they do, how reliably they do it, and where their limits are — not treated as either magic or a threat.
