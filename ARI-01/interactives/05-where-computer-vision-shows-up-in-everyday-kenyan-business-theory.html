Where Computer Vision Shows Up in Everyday Kenyan Business

Number-Plate Recognition at the Gate

Many offices, estates and malls now use gate cameras that read a vehicle's number plate straight from a photo and match it automatically against an approved list or entry log. Instead of a guard typing every plate by hand, the system reads the characters from the image and logs the entry — a direct, everyday use of the pixel-pattern reading Brian met in the first lesson of this unit.

Stock-Shelf Audits and Quality Checks

Retail branches and warehouses can have staff photograph a shelf or a batch of products, and a computer vision tool flags empty gaps, misplaced items, or visibly damaged packaging far faster than someone walking every aisle by hand. A bottling line, for example, might photograph each bottle and flag any with a crooked label or a missing cap before it ever reaches a customer.

Key Point. In each of these everyday uses, computer vision acts as a fast first-pass check that flags what needs a closer look — Rafiki Insights' clients still keep a person making the final call on anything unusual, especially where money, identity or safety is involved.

ID and Photo Verification During Onboarding

Banks, SACCOs and mobile-money agents onboarding a new customer often need to check that a submitted ID photo is clear, genuine, and matches a selfie taken at the same time. A computer vision check can do this as a fast first pass — flagging a blurry photo, a mismatch, or a suspicious image — before a staff member reviews anything unusual by hand. This speeds up onboarding without removing human judgement from decisions that matter.
