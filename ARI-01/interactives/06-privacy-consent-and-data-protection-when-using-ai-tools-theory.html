Privacy, Consent and Data Protection When Using AI Tools

Client Data Is Not Brian's to Share

When a client hires Rafiki Insights, they hand over sales figures, customer lists, or staff records so Brian can help them understand their business — not so that data can end up anywhere else. That data usually belongs partly to the client and partly to the real people it describes: their customers, their employees, their suppliers. Anyone Brian shares it with, including an AI tool, is effectively a new place that data now sits.

Public AI Tools Are Not a Private Filing Cabinet

Many free, public AI chat tools may store, log, or even learn from whatever is typed into them. Pasting a client's raw customer spreadsheet — full of names, phone numbers, or financial details — into a tool like that is, in effect, handing that data to a third party the client never agreed to share it with. It does not matter that Brian's intention was simply to get quick help; the confidentiality breach happens the moment the data leaves Rafiki Insights' hands without permission.

Key Point. If it is not obvious the client has agreed to it, do not paste identifying client data into a public AI tool. Anonymize it first, or ask permission.

Anonymization and Consent, in Practice

Anonymized data has identifying details removed or replaced — names swapped for "Customer A," exact addresses dropped, ID numbers taken out — so the useful patterns remain but no real person can be picked out. Consent means the client has clearly agreed, ideally in writing, to their data being processed by a named external tool for a specific purpose. Before Brian uses any AI tool on client data, the safe habit is: strip out anything identifying, check what the tool's terms say about storing or reusing input, and when in doubt, ask the client first.
