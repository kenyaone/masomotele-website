AI in Industry · Topic 1

Automating Repetitive Analysis Tasks

The Grunt Work Behind Every Analysis

Before Brian, a junior analyst at Rafiki Insights, gets anywhere near telling a client what their numbers actually mean, there is usually a pile of grunt work to get through first. A client hands over months of mpesa statements or till records, and someone has to clean up the messy entries, sort hundreds of transactions into sensible categories, and pull together a rough first draft of what happened that month.

None of this is difficult work exactly — it is repetitive. The same kind of decision (is this "fuel", "stock", or "rent"?) gets made hundreds of times over. For a small consultancy like Rafiki Insights, this grunt work can eat up most of a morning before the real analysis even starts.

Where Automation Earns Its Keep

This is exactly the kind of work automation and AI tools are good at: spotting patterns and repeating a rule very quickly. A tool can scan a list of transactions and group similar-looking ones together, flag entries that look like duplicates, or produce a rough first-pass paragraph summarising what the data shows — all in a fraction of the time it would take Brian to do it by hand.

That speed matters, but not because it produces a finished answer. It matters because it clears the repetitive part off Brian's desk quickly, leaving him more time for the part of the job that actually needs a person: deciding what the numbers mean for this particular client's business.

Key Point. Automation is there to clear the repetitive work off Brian's desk, not to make the final call — the judgement part of the job stays firmly human.

What Still Needs Brian

An automated first pass is a starting point, not a finished product. A tool might label a supplier payment as "personal expense" simply because the description looked similar to one it had seen before, with no idea that this client's supplier happens to share a name with a local shop. Brian still needs to check that the categorisation actually makes sense for this specific business, sanity-check the totals against what he already knows about the client, and decide which points are actually worth telling them.

Used this way, automation does not replace Brian's judgement — it buys him time to use it on the parts of the job where it counts.
