Establishing Patient Dietary Needs

What Are Dietary Needs?

Dietary needs are the specific nutritional and feeding requirements a client has because of their health condition, age, culture, or personal circumstances. For a caregiver, establishing them is the essential first step before any meal is planned — feeding a client without first understanding these needs can do real harm, such as giving sugary food to a diabetic client or dairy to someone who is lactose intolerant.

Dietary Assessment Tools

Several simple tools help a caregiver capture a client's true eating pattern rather than relying on memory alone. A 24-hour dietary recall asks the client or family to describe everything eaten in the last day. A food record profile is a written log kept over several days as meals happen. A food frequency questionnaire asks how often particular foods are eaten over weeks or months, useful for spotting patterns. A screener's profile is a short checklist used to quickly flag obvious risk factors, such as sudden weight loss or chewing difficulty, that need closer follow-up.

Key Point. Family members often know a client's habits, preferences and history best — involve them wherever possible when gathering this information.

Common Special Dietary Needs

Some clients need particular attention: diabetic clients need consistent, controlled sugar and carbohydrate intake; clients with allergies must have the triggering food strictly avoided; lactose intolerance means dairy products cause discomfort and should be limited or replaced; low blood sugar or low electrolyte needs mean a client is at risk of dizziness or weakness between meals and may need more frequent small meals; other clients may follow a vegetarian, ketogenic, or low-carb diet by medical advice or personal choice.

Key Point. A caregiver's job is to recognise and record these needs accurately, not to diagnose them — any new or unexplained symptom should be reported to the nurse or family, not self-treated.

Oral vs Tube Feeding

Most clients eat by mouth (oral feeding), but some cannot swallow safely and are fed through a tube instead. A nasogastric tube passes through the nose into the stomach, usually for shorter-term use, while a gastrostomy tube is placed directly into the stomach through the abdomen for longer-term feeding. Tube feeding is set up and supervised by qualified health staff — a caregiver's role is to follow the specific instructions given, not to decide on or change the feeding route.
