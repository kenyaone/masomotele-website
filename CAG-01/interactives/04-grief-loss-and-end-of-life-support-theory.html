Grief, Loss and End-of-Life Support

No Single "Right" Way to Grieve

Grief looks different for different people — anger, numbness, denial, or even occasional laughter can all be genuine parts of a real grieving process. Njeri should avoid assuming there is one "correct" way someone should be reacting.

Being Present Without Filling Silence

Sitting quietly with someone who is grieving, without needing to fill every silence with words or reassurance, can itself be deeply supportive — the instinct to "say something helpful" is not always necessary or even helpful.

Key Point. Comfortable silence, genuinely offered, communicates "I am here with you" more powerfully than searching for the perfect words often does.

Supporting Family Alongside the Client

In end-of-life situations, family members are often also grieving or anxious — Njeri's support role can extend to acknowledging their experience too, not solely focusing on the client and overlooking the family entirely.

Recognising When to Involve More Support

Signs like prolonged inability to function, expressions of hopelessness beyond typical grief, or mentions of self-harm are signals to involve additional professional support (a counsellor, doctor, or supervisor) — this is beyond what a caregiver alone should try to fully address.
