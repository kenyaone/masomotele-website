Module 8 · Lesson 8.2

Communicating with Special Needs Clients

Speak Directly to the Client

Directing questions and conversation to the client themselves — not to a family member standing nearby "on their behalf" — respects their autonomy, even if a family member sometimes needs to help interpret or clarify the response afterward.

Key Point. Speaking about a client in the third person while they are present, as if they cannot hear or understand, is one of the most common and avoidable disrespects in special needs care.

Allowing Extra Time

Some clients need more time to process a question or formulate a response — rushing or finishing their sentences for them (even with good intentions) can feel disrespectful and undermine their independence.

Alternative Communication Methods

Picture cards, simple gestures, written notes, or assistive communication devices may work better than spoken conversation alone for some clients — being willing to use whatever method actually works for that specific person is more important than sticking to a single "normal" approach.

Checking Understanding Respectfully

Confirming a client has understood something ("just to make sure I explained that clearly — does that make sense?") can be done respectfully, without a condescending tone that implies doubt about their basic capability.
