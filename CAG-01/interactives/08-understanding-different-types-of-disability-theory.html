Understanding Different Types of Disability

Broad Categories

Disabilities are often grouped as physical (affecting movement/mobility), sensory (affecting sight or hearing), intellectual (affecting learning/cognitive processing), or psychosocial (related to mental health conditions) — useful as a starting orientation, though many people experience a combination or something that does not fit neatly into one box.

Person-First Language

Saying "a person with a disability" rather than "a disabled person" (person-first language) is generally preferred, since it emphasises the person before the condition — though individual preference varies, and asking a client's own preference is always the best approach.

Key Point. When in doubt, asking a client how they prefer to be described is more respectful and more accurate than assuming based on general convention alone.

Avoiding Assumptions from a Label Alone

Two people with the same diagnosis can have very different actual support needs, abilities, and preferences — treating a diagnosis label as if it fully describes the person risks real, avoidable mistakes in care.

Individual Needs Come First

Getting to know each specific client's actual abilities, preferences, and support needs — rather than applying a generic template based on their category of disability — is what genuinely person-centred care looks like.
