Module 9 · Lesson 9.6

Recording Vital Signs and Care Notes

Why Charting Matters

A chart is often the only way the next shift's caregiver, a nurse, or a doctor learns what happened while you were with the client — an inaccurate or missing entry can mean a real problem goes unnoticed by everyone who reads that chart after you.

Key Point. If it was not charted, as far as the next caregiver is concerned, it did not happen.

Recording Accurately

Chart the actual instrument reading — not a rounded guess, not what you remember an hour later. Record the correct units (°C, bpm, breaths/min, mmHg) and the actual time you took the reading, not when you got around to writing it down.

Interpreting What You Recorded

Compare each value against the normal reference range. A reading squarely within normal range needs only routine documentation. A reading outside normal range but not acutely dangerous calls for closer monitoring and a recheck soon. A reading — or combination of readings — clearly outside safe limits needs to be reported immediately, not just written down for someone to notice later.

Key Point. The category you choose (Normal / Watch Closely / Report Immediately) is itself a real clinical judgement, not just paperwork.

Writing a Clear Care Note

A genuinely useful note includes the time, the specific values (not just "vitals taken"), what the client said or how they appeared, and — if anything was concerning — exactly what you did about it and who you told. A vague note like "checked on client, seemed okay" tells the next reader almost nothing.
