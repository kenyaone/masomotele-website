Module 9 · Lesson 9.5

Reporting and Escalating Concerns

Routine vs Concerning Changes

Not every change needs urgent escalation — a client having a slightly quieter day is often routine. But a sudden change in alertness, new confusion, unusual pain, a fall, or a significant change in vital signs is concerning and needs to be reported, not just monitored silently.

Who to Report To and How Quickly

Depending on severity, this might mean informing a supervisor, the client's family, a nurse, or in an emergency, calling emergency services directly. More severe or sudden changes need faster escalation — a gradual, mild change might wait for the next scheduled check-in, but an acute change should not.

Key Point. Matching the urgency of reporting to the actual severity of what was observed is a real skill — under-reacting to something serious is just as much a mistake as overreacting to something routine.

Documenting Clearly

A clear report includes what was observed, when, and any relevant context — "he seemed confused around 2pm and didn't recognise me for a few minutes" is far more useful than "something seemed off today".

Silence Is Not a Safe Default

Staying silent about a genuine concern because "I didn't want to bother anyone" or "it's probably nothing" is a common but risky instinct — reporting a concern that turns out to be minor costs little; missing a real one can cost a great deal.
