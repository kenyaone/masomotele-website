Cabling Standards and Connectors

Coax (Analog) Cabling

Older and analog CCTV systems typically use coaxial cable — commonly RG59 or RG6 — to carry the analog video signal from a camera to a DVR (digital video recorder). This cable terminates in a BNC connector at each end, and in most analog setups the camera also needs a separate power cable, since standard coax alone does not carry power to the camera.

Coax-based systems are simple, well understood, and still common on older or budget analog installs, but each camera needs two runs (video and power) unless a combined power/video cable is used.

UTP / PoE (IP) Cabling

Modern IP camera systems typically use UTP network cable (Cat5e or Cat6) to carry a digital video signal to an NVR (network video recorder), terminating in an RJ45 connector at each end — the same connector used for ordinary computer network cabling.

Power over Ethernet (PoE) allows both power and data to travel over that same single Ethernet cable, so an IP camera can be installed with just one cable run instead of two. This simplifies installation, reduces cabling clutter, and IP cameras generally support higher resolutions than analog coax cameras.

Cable Quality and Length Limits

Cheap or poor-quality cable, or a run that is too long for the cable type, causes signal degradation and interference: symptoms range from a flickering or snowy analog picture to a dropped, unreliable, or completely dead IP camera connection.

Both coax and standard Ethernet/PoE cabling have practical maximum reliable run lengths (standard Ethernet-based PoE is generally reliable up to roughly 100 metres before signal loss becomes a problem, though repeaters, switches, or fibre can extend a run beyond that). A professional installer plans cable routes and lengths in advance and uses properly rated cable, rather than discovering a length or quality problem after the camera is already mounted.

Key Point. Match the cabling to the camera and system type — coax with BNC for analog DVR systems, UTP/PoE with RJ45 for IP/NVR systems — and never skimp on cable quality or ignore length limits, since both directly affect how reliable the final picture will be.
