Lenses, Resolution and Field of View

Fixed vs Varifocal Lenses

A fixed lens has one set focal length: the zoom level and field of view are decided when the lens is manufactured and cannot be changed on site. Fixed lenses are simple and cost-effective, and work well when the distance and angle to what needs recording are already known and unlikely to change — a corridor camera pointed at a single fixed door, for example.

A varifocal lens lets the installer adjust the focal length by hand at the time of installation, zooming the view in or out to fine-tune exactly how much area the camera covers before locking it in place. This makes varifocal lenses more flexible during a site survey, at a somewhat higher cost than an equivalent fixed lens.

Understanding Resolution (2MP, 4MP...)

Resolution describes how many pixels make up each recorded image, usually stated in megapixels (MP) — a 2MP camera records roughly 2 million pixels per frame, a 4MP camera roughly 4 million. More pixels mean a sharper image, which matters most when someone later needs to zoom into a recording to identify a face, a vehicle, or a number plate.

Higher resolution is not free, however: sharper images produce larger video files, which means more storage space on the recorder and more network bandwidth to move that video around. A system designer has to balance how much detail is actually needed against the storage and bandwidth budget available.

Field of View Trade-offs

Field of view (FOV) is how much of a scene a camera captures at once. A wide field of view takes in a large area, but every object within it appears smaller and less detailed. A narrow, more zoomed-in field of view shows a smaller area in much greater detail, useful for tasks like reading a face or a plate clearly at a distance.

Because a lens cannot maximise both coverage and detail at the same time, choosing field of view means deciding what the camera is really for: broad overview monitoring of an area, or close, detailed identification of something specific happening within it.

Key Point. Choose resolution and field of view together around the actual goal: higher resolution with a narrower field of view at a spot where faces or plates must be identified, and a more modest resolution with a wider field of view for general overview of a large area where storage matters more than fine detail.
