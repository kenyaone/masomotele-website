Camera Placement and Coverage Planning

Priority Areas First

With the site survey notes in hand, Dennis starts placement by marking the areas that matter most: every entrance and exit, tills or cash points, storage of high-value stock, and the main perimeter access points identified in the survey. These get first claim on camera positions, before anything else is considered.

Only once the priority areas are properly covered does Dennis look at secondary areas — general aisles, corridors, or open yard space — and even then, only if the client's budget and risk level justify it.

Avoiding Overlap and Wasted Coverage

A common beginner mistake is pointing two cameras at the same empty corridor "just to be safe," while a genuine blind spot near a side door goes uncovered. Every camera should earn its position by covering something no other camera already sees well. Some overlap at key chokepoints (like a main entrance) is reasonable, but overlap across low-risk open space is simply wasted budget.

Key Point. Every camera should earn its place covering a real need — not just get added because a client thinks "more cameras must mean more security."

Mounting Height and Angle

Indoors, Dennis typically mounts cameras around 2.4 to 2.7 metres — high enough to be out of easy reach for tampering, but low enough to still capture usable facial detail at entrances and tills. Outdoors, cameras often go higher, around 3 to 4 metres, trading some facial detail for a wider field of view and better resistance to vandalism. The angle matters too — a camera should be angled to capture faces and activity, not just the tops of heads or an empty patch of floor.
