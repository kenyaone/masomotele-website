Storage Options: DVR/NVR Capacity Planning

What Drives Storage Use Up or Down

How much hard drive space a client needs depends on a handful of settings working together: the camera's resolution (megapixels), its frame rate (frames per second), the compression codec used (H.264 or the newer H.265), and how many cameras are recording. Push any of these up and the daily storage use per camera goes up with it.

H.265 is worth knowing well: for similar image quality, it typically compresses video to roughly half the size of H.264, which can significantly reduce the drive size Dennis needs to recommend.

Continuous Recording vs Motion-Only

Recording continuously, 24 hours a day, uses far more storage than recording only when the cameras detect motion. Motion-triggered recording might only capture footage during a fraction of the day, saving a lot of drive space — but there is a small risk of missing something if a slow-moving subject doesn't trigger the motion sensor reliably.

Key Point. A simple way to estimate: work out one camera's daily storage use in gigabytes, multiply by the number of cameras, then multiply by the number of days of retention the client wants.

Picking the Right Drive and Retention Period

Clients often ask for a specific retention period, such as 30 days, for legal or insurance reasons. Once Dennis has estimated the total storage needed for that period, he picks a hard drive size that comfortably covers it with some margin to spare. For the drive itself, surveillance-rated models (like WD Purple or Seagate SkyHawk) are built for continuous 24/7 read/write duty, unlike an ordinary desktop hard drive that isn't designed to run that hard non-stop. On larger installs with many cameras, an NVR with multiple drive bays or RAID redundancy is worth considering.
