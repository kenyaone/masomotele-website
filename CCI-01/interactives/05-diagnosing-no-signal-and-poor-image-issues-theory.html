Module 5 · Lesson 5.1

Diagnosing No-Signal and Poor-Image Issues

1. Start With Power

Before Dennis suspects a broken camera, he checks the simplest thing first: does the camera actually have power? For a PoE camera, that means checking the switch or NVR port's PoE status light, or trying a known-good PoE injector. For a camera on a separate power supply, it means checking the adapter, the fuse, and the cable for visible damage.

A camera with no power will always show "no signal" — but so will a camera with a bad cable. Checking power first is quick, and it rules out the single most common cause before anything else is touched.

2. Follow the Cable Path and Connectors

If power is confirmed, Dennis follows the cable from the camera to the recorder, looking for kinks, cuts, chew marks, or water ingress at outdoor joins. He also checks each connector — a BNC or RJ45 end that was poorly crimped, or has started to corrode, can cause exactly the same symptoms as a dead camera: no signal, or a signal that flickers and drops.

Where possible, Dennis tests with a known-good cable or connector on the same run. If the picture returns, the fault was in the cable path, not the camera itself.

3. Isolate Camera vs Recorder

If power and cabling both check out, the last step is a simple swap test. Dennis connects the suspect camera to a different, known-good channel or port. If it now works, the fault is on the recorder side — a bad port, a channel setting, or the recorder itself. If it still fails, the camera is the problem.

The reverse test works too: connect a known-good camera to the suspect channel. If that camera also fails to show, the recorder side is confirmed as the cause, not the original camera.

Key Point. Always work outward from the power source — power → cable → connector → camera → recorder. Most "no signal" calls turn out to be a power or connector problem, not a failed camera.
