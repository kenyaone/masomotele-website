AI-Powered Motion Detection and Smart Alerts

Basic Motion Detection vs Smart Detection

A basic CCTV motion sensor (a PIR sensor or simple pixel-change detection on the camera image) reacts to any change in the scene — it has no idea what actually moved. A branch swaying in the wind, a shadow shifting as clouds pass, a moth flying past the lens at night, or a cat crossing the yard will all trigger exactly the same alert as a person climbing over a fence.

AI-based "smart detection" (sometimes called smart motion, human/vehicle detection, or AI analytics, depending on the brand) adds a classification step. Before an alert is raised, the camera or NVR runs the moving object through a small AI model that tries to answer: is this a person, a vehicle, an animal, or just background movement? Only object types the installer has chosen to alert on will actually generate a notification.

Why This Matters On Site

False alarms are one of the biggest reasons clients lose trust in a CCTV system. If a homeowner's phone buzzes every time a tree moves or their cat walks past, they quickly start ignoring every alert — including the one that matters. Smart detection exists to cut that noise down, but it only works well if it is configured properly for the actual property.

Smart detection is not magic — sensitivity still matters, detection zones still matter, and the camera still needs a reasonably clear, well-lit view of the area to classify objects reliably. A poorly aimed or badly lit camera can still produce false or missed detections even with AI features switched on.

Key point. AI smart detection filters what triggered the alert (person, vehicle, animal), not just that something moved — but it still needs correct zone and sensitivity setup by the installer to actually work well.

Setting It Up Sensibly For a Client

When Dennis sets up a system at a SafeView Security Systems client, his usual habit is: draw detection zones that cover the areas that actually matter (gates, doors, driveways) rather than the whole frame, keep tree lines and busy public roads out of the alert zone where possible, and choose object filters that match what the client actually wants to know about — usually person and vehicle for a home, sometimes animal too if livestock or pets are a real concern for that property.

He also talks to the client about what "normal" movement looks like on their property (a gardener who visits twice a week, a dog that has the run of the yard) so the system is tuned to their actual life, not a generic default.
