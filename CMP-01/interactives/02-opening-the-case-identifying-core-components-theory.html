Opening the Case — Identifying Core Components

Physical practicum required. This step teaches the judgement and correct sequence for a real hands-on repair task. To be certified competent in the physical technique itself (not just the decision-making), you must complete a supervised in-person practicum session — this online step alone does not certify hands-on ability.

1. Opening the case

Most desktop cases open via 2–4 thumbscrews or Phillips screws on the back panel, releasing one or both side panels. Some newer cases use tool-less latches. Once open, resist the urge to touch anything until you've visually mapped out what you're looking at.

2. The component tour

ComponentHow to spot it
MotherboardThe large flat green/black board everything else plugs into.
CPUHidden under a cooler/heatsink+fan near the center of the motherboard — you rarely see the chip itself unless the cooler is removed.
RAMThin vertical sticks in slots right next to the CPU socket, usually 2–4 slots.
GPUThe largest expansion card, usually with its own fan(s), plugged into the topmost long PCIe slot.
Storage (SSD/HDD)SSDs: small flat sticks (M.2) mounted directly on the motherboard, or 2.5" boxes. HDDs: larger 3.5" metal boxes mounted in drive bays.
PSUA metal box, usually top or bottom rear of the case, with a bundle of cables fanning out to everything else.
P

Peter always does a "3-second scan" before touching anything: PSU, motherboard, CPU cooler, RAM, GPU, storage — in that order, top to bottom. It becomes second nature and helps you spot what's missing or obviously wrong (a fan not spinning, a cable unplugged) before you even run a single test.

3. Form factors

The motherboard's physical size and mounting-hole pattern is its form factor — it determines which cases it fits in.

  • ATX — the standard full-size desktop board (≈305×244mm)
  • Micro-ATX — smaller, fewer expansion slots (≈244×244mm)
  • Mini-ITX — compact, usually one expansion slot, common in small-form-factor builds (≈170×170mm)

4. Laptop internals — what's different

Laptops cram the same logical components into a fraction of the space: RAM is often soldered or uses small SO-DIMM slots; storage is M.2 or 2.5"; there's no separate PSU (a power brick outside feeds an internal charging/power circuit instead); and the CPU/GPU are often soldered directly rather than socketed. This is why laptop repair generally requires more precision and more caution with fragile ribbon cables.
