Motherboard Visual Inspection and Common Failures

Physical practicum required. This step teaches the judgement and correct sequence for a real hands-on repair task. To be certified competent in the physical technique itself (not just the decision-making), you must complete a supervised in-person practicum session — this online step alone does not certify hands-on ability.

1. Bulging or leaking capacitors

Capacitors are small cylindrical components scattered across the motherboard. A healthy capacitor has a flat top; a failing one bulges upward, domes, or visibly leaks a crusty residue. This is one of the single most reliable visual signs of motherboard failure, and it is often the actual cause behind vague symptoms like random reboots or instability that never got properly diagnosed.

Bulging capacitors do not repair themselves and rarely get better. A board showing this should be flagged for replacement, not just monitored.

2. Burn marks and discoloration

Dark scorch marks, melted plastic near a connector, or a burning smell are signs of a short circuit or component failure that generated excess heat. This kind of damage is essentially never repairable at the component level by a general technician, and points straight to a board replacement.

3. Physical damage around connectors and slots

Bent pins in a CPU socket, cracked PCIe slot plastic, or a snapped RAM slot retention clip are common results of rough handling, and each affects a specific function without necessarily killing the whole board. Careful visual inspection under good light, sometimes with a magnifier, catches these before you waste time chasing a software explanation for what is actually a physical fault.

4. Repair vs replace

Component-level motherboard repair, such as replacing a single failed capacitor, is possible but requires specialised soldering skill and equipment most general repair shops do not have. For most real-world jobs, a motherboard showing serious physical damage is quoted for replacement rather than attempted repair -- the labour cost of a skilled component-level repair often exceeds the cost of a replacement board.
