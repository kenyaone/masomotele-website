Switches, Hubs and Routers

1. Hubs -- mostly a history lesson now

A hub is the simplest, oldest device: it repeats every incoming signal out to every other port, with no intelligence at all. This wastes bandwidth and creates unnecessary collisions, which is why hubs have been almost entirely replaced by switches in real networks today. Still worth recognising, since older documentation and some legacy environments reference them.

2. Switches

A switch is smarter: it learns which device (by MAC address) is connected to which port, and sends traffic only to the specific port that device is on, not to everyone. This is far more efficient and is the standard device for connecting multiple devices within a LAN today. Switches operate primarily at Layer 2 (Data Link), using MAC addresses to make forwarding decisions.

3. Routers

A router connects different networks together and makes forwarding decisions based on IP addresses, operating at Layer 3 (Network). A router is what lets a home or office LAN reach the internet (a different network), and what lets an organisation's separate LANs communicate with each other. Routers, not switches, are what actually route traffic between networks.

Simple rule: a switch connects devices WITHIN one network; a router connects DIFFERENT networks together. Most home "routers" are actually combo devices doing both jobs in one box.
