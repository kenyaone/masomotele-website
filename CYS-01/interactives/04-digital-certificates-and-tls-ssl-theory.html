Digital Certificates and TLS/SSL

What a Certificate Proves

A digital certificate binds a public key to a specific identity (e.g. "safaripay.co.ke"), digitally signed by a trusted third party. When a customer's browser connects to SafariPay's site, the certificate lets the browser verify it is actually talking to the real SafariPay, not an impostor.

Certificate Authorities

A Certificate Authority (CA) is a trusted organisation that verifies a domain's ownership before issuing a certificate for it, then digitally signs that certificate. Browsers trust certificates signed by recognised CAs — this is the "chain of trust" that makes the whole system work.

Key Point. The padlock icon in a browser means "this connection is encrypted and the site's identity was verified by a trusted CA" — it does NOT mean "this site is trustworthy or safe to use" in a broader sense.

What HTTPS/TLS Protects

HTTPS (HTTP over TLS) encrypts traffic between browser and server, and verifies the server's identity via its certificate. It does not verify the site's content is honest or safe — a phishing site can also obtain a valid certificate for its own (different, deceptive) domain name.

Certificate Expiry

Certificates have an expiry date and must be renewed periodically. An expired certificate causes browsers to show prominent warnings, and technically means the identity verification is no longer considered current — a lapse SafariPay must actively track and prevent.
