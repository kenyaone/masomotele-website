Log Review and Incident Triage

Why the Log Entry Itself Matters

A Tier 2 analyst, Grace's manager, or an auditor may only ever see the log entry, not the original raw alert — an inaccurate or vague entry means whoever reads it later has to redo the investigation from scratch, or worse, misses something entirely.

Key Point. The log entry is often the only surviving record of what an analyst actually saw and decided — accuracy here has real downstream consequences.

Reading the Indicators

Four fields do most of the work in a first-pass triage: the account involved (a routine user vs. a privileged admin account), the source (a known office IP vs. an unrecognised or foreign one), the timing (business hours vs. the middle of the night), and any outbound data transfer (none vs. an unusually large one). No single field alone tells the whole story — it is the combination that matters.

Triaging Severity

Benign fits a pattern with an ordinary explanation (one failed login, then success, from a known location during business hours). Monitor fits something unusual but not clearly malicious (a new location, outside normal hours, but otherwise normal behaviour) — verify and watch, rather than dismiss or panic. Escalate Immediately fits multiple strong indicators appearing together (a burst of failed attempts, an unrecognised foreign source, success, then a large outbound transfer).

Key Point. This mirrors the same "Normal / Watch Closely / Report Immediately" judgement used in other caregiving-style contexts — three tiers of response, matched to genuine severity, not treated identically.

Writing a Clear Incident Note

A genuinely useful note includes the time, the specific values that drove the decision (not just "suspicious activity"), the reasoning behind the triage category chosen, and what action was actually taken — including who was told, if anyone. A vague note like "checked the alert, looked fine" gives the next reader almost nothing to work with.
