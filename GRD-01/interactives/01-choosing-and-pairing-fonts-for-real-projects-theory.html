Choosing and Pairing Fonts for Real Projects

The Four Font Categories

Every font Ivy picks at Chapa Design Studio falls roughly into one of four categories. Serif fonts (like Georgia or Times) have small finishing strokes at the ends of letters and feel traditional and trustworthy — a solid choice for printed body text. Sans-serif fonts (like Helvetica or Poppins) have no finishing strokes, and read as clean and modern — a safe default for screens, UI and small text.

Script fonts imitate handwriting or calligraphy and feel personal or elegant — great for a single word like “Invitation”, but exhausting to read in long blocks. Display fonts are bold, decorative, headline-only fonts designed to grab attention at a large size on a poster or logo, never meant for paragraphs of text.

Readability at Different Sizes

A font that looks stunning as a giant headline can fall apart at body-text size — thin decorative details simply disappear once the letters get small. The reverse is also true: a plain, sturdy body font can look flat and boring blown up to headline size. The fix is simple: always check a font at the actual size it will be used at, not just zoomed in on a screen.

This matters even more in print. A flyer that looks fine on Ivy’s monitor can render differently once it is printed at true size, so a quick printed proof of the smallest text is always worth it before a big print run.

Pairing Fonts That Don’t Clash

The safest approach on a real project is to pick one font for headlines and a different font for body text, and to make sure the two clearly contrast rather than compete — for example, a bold display headline with a plain sans-serif body, or an elegant serif headline with a simple sans-serif body. Most projects at Chapa Design Studio never need more than two font families; a third is sometimes used only for a tiny accent like a logo mark.

Key Point. If two fonts feel almost the same but not quite, they clash. Pair fonts that are clearly different from each other (a decorative headline with a plain body font) rather than two similar general-purpose fonts.
