Typography Hierarchy: Headlines, Body Text and Emphasis

What Visual Hierarchy Means

Visual hierarchy is the order in which a design guides a viewer’s eye — what they notice first, second and last. On a flyer, that usually means: headline first, supporting details next, fine print last. Hierarchy is created by making some elements more visually prominent than others, not by hoping the reader finds everything on their own.

Building Hierarchy with Size, Weight and Spacing

Bigger text pulls the eye first, so the single most important line — usually the event name or headline — should be the largest thing on the page. Bolder weight signals importance without needing to change size at all. Spacing groups related information together: tightening the gap between a heading and the paragraph under it visually ties them together, while extra space separates unrelated blocks.

On a typical event flyer, Ivy might set the event name huge and bold, the date and time in a medium, regular weight, and the terms and conditions in small, quiet type at the bottom — three clear levels the eye can move through in order.

Using Emphasis Sparingly

Bold, italics, underlines and color are all forms of emphasis, and they only work if they are used for the few words that truly need to stand out. The moment everything on a page is emphasized, none of it stands out anymore, and the layout starts to feel noisy and unplanned. The same discipline applies to font styles overall: sticking to two or three styles per project (rather than a new font or weight for every line) keeps a design looking intentional and professional.

Key Point. If everything on the page is bold, nothing is bold. Reserve strong emphasis for the one or two things you most want a viewer to notice first.
