Building a Brand Color Palette

Primary, Secondary, and Accent Colors

A brand palette is usually built from three roles, not just a pile of colors. The primary color is the one most associated with the brand — it appears the most and usually anchors the logo. The secondary color supports it, used for backgrounds, secondary text, or supporting graphics. The accent color is used sparingly, for buttons, highlights, or calls to action, precisely because it stands out from the rest of the palette.

Ivy assigns each color a clear job before she ever opens CorelDraw, so she isn't guessing which color goes where on every new piece.

Why Fewer Colors Work Better

It's tempting to reach for a new color every time something looks a little flat, but a client's brand palette usually works best with just three to five colors total. A tight palette is easier to apply consistently across a logo, a flyer, a signboard, and a social post — and it's far easier for a client's own staff to reuse correctly without a designer double-checking every file.

If Ivy finds herself needing a sixth or seventh color, that's usually a sign the layout has a different problem that more colors won't fix.

Consistency Builds Recognition

The real power of a brand palette isn't any single color — it's using the exact same colors, the same way, every single time. A customer who sees the same shade of orange on a shopfront sign, a receipt, and a Facebook post starts to recognize the brand almost instantly, even before reading a single word. Swapping in a "close enough" shade of blue on one job breaks that recognition, even if nobody can say exactly what changed.

Key Point. A brand palette only works if it's used exactly the same way everywhere — write down the exact color values so "close enough" never has to happen.
