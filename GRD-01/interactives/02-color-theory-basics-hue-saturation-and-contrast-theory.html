Color Theory Basics: Hue, Saturation, and Contrast

Hue, Saturation, and Brightness: The Building Blocks

Every color Ivy picks in CorelDraw is really three things layered together: hue, saturation, and brightness. Hue is the base color itself — red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple — and it's what you see arranged around the color wheel. Saturation is how intense or muted that hue is: a highly saturated red is bold and vivid, while a low-saturation red edges toward a dusty, greyish pink. Brightness (sometimes called value) is how light or dark the color is, from a near-white tint down to a near-black shade.

Changing any one of the three gives you a completely different color, even if the underlying hue stays the same. Understanding this is what lets Ivy adjust a color deliberately instead of endlessly guessing at random swatches.

Warm Colors vs Cool Colors

Warm colors — reds, oranges, and yellows — sit on one side of the color wheel and tend to feel energetic, urgent, or inviting; they're a common choice for sale banners and food branding. Cool colors — blues, greens, and purples — sit on the opposite side and tend to feel calm, trustworthy, or professional, which is why so many banks and tech companies lean on blue.

Neither is "better." The right choice depends on the mood the client's brand needs to communicate. Ivy always asks herself: does this piece need to feel exciting, or does it need to feel steady and reliable?

Contrast: Why Some Text Is Hard to Read

Contrast is the difference in brightness between two colors placed next to each other, and it's what makes text readable rather than a blur. Light grey text on a white background might look "elegant" in a mockup, but on a real screen or printed flyer, low contrast means people squint or simply give up reading.

As a rule, light text needs a genuinely dark background, and dark text needs a genuinely light background — pairing two mid-tone colors of similar brightness is one of the most common readability mistakes new designers make.

Key Point. If you can't tell at a glance whether text is easy to read, it probably isn't. Light text always needs a properly dark background — "a little darker" usually isn't dark enough.
