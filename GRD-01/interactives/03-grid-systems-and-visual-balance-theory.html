Grid Systems and Visual Balance

What a Grid Actually Is

A layout grid is an invisible structure of columns, margins and gutters that sits underneath a design before any text or image is placed on it. Instead of deciding where each element goes from scratch, Ivy first divides the flyer into columns of equal width, separated by consistent gaps (gutters), with margins framing the whole page. Every element she then places lines up against this hidden scaffolding.

The grid does not show up in the final printed flyer — the viewer never sees the columns themselves — but they feel the effect of it. A layout built on a grid reads as organised and deliberate, even to someone who has never heard the word "grid" in their life.

Aligning to the Grid

The real value of a grid appears once Ivy starts aligning elements to it. If a headline's left edge lines up exactly with a photo's left edge two rows below, the layout feels connected and intentional. If it is a few millimetres off, most viewers cannot say exactly what is wrong, but the layout will feel slightly untidy — as if something does not quite line up, because it doesn't.

This is why grid alignment matters even for small, subtle offsets: the eye is very sensitive to consistent versus inconsistent alignment, even when it cannot name the difference.

Balance Without Symmetry

Balance is not the same thing as symmetry. A symmetrical layout mirrors itself left-to-right or top-to-bottom. A balanced layout simply distributes visual weight evenly, and visual weight comes from size, color, contrast and density — not just position.

Suppose Ivy's flyer has a large dark logo on the left and mostly empty space on the right. Instead of mirroring the logo (which would feel rigid and repetitive), she can balance it with a smaller but visually "heavy" element on the right — a bold price tag, a strong color block, a short punchy line of text. The two sides are different, but the layout still feels settled rather than lopsided.

Key Point. A grid is not a straightjacket — it is the invisible structure that lets Ivy place elements confidently and consistently, so the layout feels intentional rather than cluttered. Balance, meanwhile, is about weight, not mirrors: uneven positions can still feel perfectly calm if the visual weight is distributed evenly.
