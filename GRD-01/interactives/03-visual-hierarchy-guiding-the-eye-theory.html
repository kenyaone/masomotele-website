Visual Hierarchy: Guiding the Eye

Why Hierarchy Matters

Visual hierarchy is the order in which a viewer's eye moves across a layout — what they notice first, second, and third. Left entirely to chance, a viewer's eye wanders randomly and may miss the one thing Ivy most needs them to see, like a discount percentage or an event date.

A designer controls this order deliberately. Every layout should have one clear "first" thing to notice, a clear "second," and so on — not everything shouting for attention at once.

Size and Position: The Fast Signals

Size is the fastest way to signal importance: a larger headline is read as more important than smaller surrounding text, almost automatically. But position matters just as much, and it acts even before size registers — elements placed where the eye naturally lands first (often the upper portion of a layout, or a spot with strong contrast) get noticed quickly even if they are physically small.

This is why a small logo, placed carefully at the top of a poster, can still be one of the first things a viewer registers, well before a larger paragraph of text below it.

Whitespace: Giving Elements Room to Breathe

Whitespace — the empty space around an element — is not wasted space. Generous whitespace around a price or a headline isolates it from everything else, which makes it easier for the eye to land on and easier for the brain to register as important. Crowding an element tightly against other content does the opposite: it buries it.

On Ivy's sale poster, giving the "50% OFF" text its own clear space, rather than squeezing it between the date and the logo, is often more effective than simply making the text bigger.

Key Point. Not every element can be the most important thing on the page — trying to make everything stand out equally usually means nothing does. Choose one clear priority, then use size, position and whitespace together to make sure that is genuinely the first thing the eye finds.
