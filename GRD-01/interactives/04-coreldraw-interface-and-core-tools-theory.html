CorelDraw Interface and Core Tools

The CorelDraw Workspace

When Ivy opens CorelDraw for the first time at Chapa Design Studio, the screen looks busy — but it breaks down into three main areas. The toolbox runs down the left edge and holds every drawing and editing tool: the Pick tool, shape tools, the Bezier tool, text tool, and more. The property bar sits just under the top menu, and it changes depending on what tool or object is currently active — for example, selecting an ellipse shows options for outline width and corner rounding that would not appear if a text box were selected. On the right edge, dockers are collapsible panels (such as Object Properties or the Color palette) that give detailed control over whatever is selected without cluttering the main canvas.

The Pick Tool

The Pick tool sits at the very top of the toolbox and is shaped like a solid black arrow. It is the default selection tool — with it, Ivy can click an object to select it, then drag to move it, drag a corner handle to resize it, or drag a rotation handle to rotate it. Almost every other tool in CorelDraw is used briefly for a specific job (drawing a shape, adding text, editing a node) and then Ivy switches back to the Pick tool to select, move, or arrange what she just created. Pressing the spacebar is a fast shortcut back to the Pick tool from most other tools.

Key Point. If Ivy ever feels lost mid-task, pressing the spacebar (or clicking the arrow icon at the top of the toolbox) brings her straight back to the Pick tool — the safe "home base" for selecting and arranging objects.

Vector vs Raster: Why CorelDraw Is Vector-First

A raster image (like a photo or a screenshot, usually a JPG or PNG) is built from a fixed grid of colored pixels. Zoom in far enough, or stretch it larger than its original size, and it turns blurry or blocky because there is no more real detail to show. A vector graphic instead stores shapes as mathematical paths — points connected by straight or curved lines, with fill and outline properties attached. Because it is math rather than a pixel grid, a vector shape can be scaled up to the size of a building wrap or down to a business card with no loss of sharpness at all.

CorelDraw's native file format (.cdr) is vector-based, and its core drawing tools — shapes, Bezier paths, text — all produce vector artwork. This is exactly why it is the industry standard for logos, signage and branding work: a client's logo drawn once in CorelDraw can be resized for a pen, a poster, or a shop front sign without ever looking pixelated.
