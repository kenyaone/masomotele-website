Patient Confidentiality and Consent

1. Confidentiality in practice

Patient information -- their diagnosis, treatment, personal circumstances, even the simple fact that they are a patient at the facility -- is confidential. This applies to conversations overheard in a corridor, files left visible on a desk, and casual mentions to family or friends outside work, not just formal medical records.

2. When confidentiality can be broken

Confidentiality is not absolute, but the exceptions are narrow and specific: a genuine legal requirement (like certain notifiable diseases), a serious risk of harm to the patient or others, or the patient's own informed consent to share. "I thought it was fine to mention" is not one of the exceptions.

3. What informed consent requires

Informed consent means a patient understands what a procedure or treatment involves, its risks and benefits, and the alternatives, before agreeing to it. Consent given without real understanding is not meaningfully informed consent, even if a form was signed.

4. This applies to support staff too

Mercy says: "I'm not a nurse or a doctor, but I still see and hear things about patients every single day. Confidentiality isn't just a clinical-staff rule -- it applies to me exactly as much as it applies to them."
