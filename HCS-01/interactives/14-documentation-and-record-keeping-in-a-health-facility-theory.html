Documentation and Record-Keeping in a Health Facility

1. "If it wasn\'t written down, it didn\'t happen"

This principle reflects a real legal and clinical reality: if care was given but never documented, there is no record it happened, which can affect both patient safety (the next caregiver doesn\'t know) and legal accountability (there\'s no evidence of what was actually done).

2. What makes a good record entry

  • Accurate: reflects exactly what happened, not an approximation or assumption
  • Objective: records observations and facts, not personal opinions or judgments
  • Timely: written close to when the event happened, while details are fresh and accurate
  • Legible and clear: genuinely usable by whoever reads it next

3. Confidentiality and security of records

Records must be kept secure -- not left visible to unauthorised people, not shared casually, and access limited to those who have a genuine need for that information as part of the patient's care.

4. Timeliness matters

Mercy says: "I try to document things as close to when they happened as I reasonably can. Waiting until the end of a long shift means I'm relying on memory, and memory is exactly where small but important details get lost or blurred together."
