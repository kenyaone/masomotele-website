Overcoming Communication Barriers

1. Common barriers Mercy encounters

BarrierExample
LanguageClient speaks a different first language, or uses limited English/Kiswahili
SensoryHearing loss, vision loss
CognitiveConfusion, dementia, effects of illness or medication
EmotionalFear, pain, distress making it hard to focus or respond

2. Strategies for language and sensory barriers

For a language barrier: speak slowly and clearly, use simple words, use gestures or pictures where helpful, and use a qualified interpreter for anything important rather than a family member guessing. For hearing loss: face the client, speak clearly without shouting, and reduce background noise. For vision loss: identify yourself by name and announce what you are about to do before touching anything.

3. Communicating with a confused client

Mercy says: "With a client who is confused, I keep my sentences short and simple, use their name often, stay calm even if they repeat themselves, and never argue with what they believe is true in the moment. Correcting them forcefully usually just increases distress, without helping anything."

4. When to pause and get help

If a communication barrier is serious enough that Mercy cannot be confident the client actually understands what is happening -- for example around consent for a procedure -- that is a moment to pause and get a qualified interpreter or senior staff member involved, not to guess and continue.
