Understanding Psychosocial Needs of Patients

1. What "psychosocial" means

Good patient care has always been about more than the physical body. "Psychosocial" combines two things: the psychological (a patient's emotions, thoughts and mental state) and the social (their relationships, roles and support network). A patient recovering physically can still be struggling badly on the psychosocial side -- frightened, lonely, or worried about things far beyond the hospital walls -- and that struggle is just as real, even though it doesn't show up on a vital signs chart.

2. Common psychosocial needs

NeedWhy it matters
Safety and securityFeeling physically and emotionally safe reduces fear and helps recovery
Dignity and privacyIllness already strips away a lot of control; preserving dignity protects self-worth
Connection and communicationIsolation from family and normal routines is itself distressing
Information and a sense of controlNot knowing what is happening to them, or why, increases anxiety

3. Recognising signs of distress

Signs that a patient may be experiencing psychosocial distress include sudden withdrawal or quietness, irritability or anger that seems out of character, noticeable changes in sleep or appetite, excessive worry, or tearfulness.

These signs are a cue to look closer and report -- not a cue to guess a diagnosis. A support worker who notices withdrawal or irritability should not assume they know the cause (grief, fear, depression, pain, confusion can all look similar); the job is to notice, respond with basic comfort, and pass the observation along clearly.

4. Mercy's support role

Mercy says: "I'm not a counsellor and I don't try to be one. What I can do is sit with someone, really listen, keep their routine steady, and make sure the right person knows if something seems wrong. That's not a small thing -- it's exactly my job."

Providing basic emotional support -- listening, reassurance, consistency, respect -- is squarely within a support worker's role. Assessing or treating a mental health condition is not, and is always referred on to a nurse, counsellor or clinician.
