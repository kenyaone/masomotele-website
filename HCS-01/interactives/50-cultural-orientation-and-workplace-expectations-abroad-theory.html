Cultural Orientation and Workplace Expectations Abroad

1. Cultural differences to expect

Every host country has its own norms around greetings and etiquette, dress codes (some countries expect modest dress in public and at work), religious practices such as set prayer times, food customs, and workplace expectations tied to gender. None of these are "wrong" simply because they differ from home -- they are part of what a worker prepares for before travelling, not something to be surprised by on arrival.

2. Workplace expectations abroad

AspectWhat may differ
Supervision styleSome workplaces have a stricter hierarchy and more formal instructions than Mercy may be used to at home
Working hoursLive-in caregiving arrangements abroad can mean longer, less predictable hours than a fixed hospital shift
Documentation standardsSome employers expect stricter, more frequent written records of care given
Mercy says: "The skills I built here -- careful observation, clear reporting, respecting scope of practice -- travel with me anywhere. The setting changes, but doing the job properly doesn't."

3. Coping with homesickness and adjustment

Feeling homesick in the first weeks is completely normal, not a sign that something has gone wrong. Staying connected with family and friends back home, seeking out a community of fellow Kenyans where possible, and giving yourself real time to adjust all genuinely help.

4. Know your rights and support channels

Keep copies of your contract and documents in a safe, separate place, know your embassy's contact details before you need them, and never assume mistreatment or a contract violation is something you simply have to accept quietly. Reporting a genuine problem, through the recruitment agency or the embassy, is the correct response -- not staying silent.
