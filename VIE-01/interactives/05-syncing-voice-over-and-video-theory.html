Audio Editing · Topic 2

Syncing Voice-Over and Video

Why Voice-Overs Get Re-Recorded

Otieno often films a client speaking on location — a shop owner giving a testimonial, a pastor greeting a congregation — only to find the on-camera audio is full of traffic noise or wind. Rather than trying to rescue the original take, he re-records the same lines in a quiet room and drops that clean voice-over onto its own audio track under the original video, muting or deleting the noisy on-camera audio.

Because the new recording was never performed in perfect lockstep with the camera, it almost never lines up with the client's lips the moment it is dropped onto the timeline — it needs to be nudged into place.

Spotting a Sync Mismatch

Otieno zooms in on the timeline and scrubs slowly across a moment where the client's mouth clearly opens or closes. If the sound arrives before the lips move, the audio is said to lead the video; if it arrives after, the audio lags behind. Either direction looks and sounds wrong to a viewer, even by a fraction of a second.

The waveform view helps here: a sharp spike in the waveform should land under the exact frame where the mouth closes or opens, not several frames before or after it.

Nudging the Audio Track

To fix a mismatch, Otieno drags the audio clip along its own track: moving it left makes it play earlier, and moving it right makes it play later. If the audio was leading (arriving too early), it needs to move right/later so it catches up with the lips; if it was lagging, it needs to move left/earlier. He zooms the timeline in tightly and nudges in small steps, checking the offset in milliseconds or frames rather than eyeballing a rough drag.

A reliable way to check the fix is to find a plosive consonant in the line — a hard "p" or "b" sound — since the lips visibly snap shut right at that instant, giving a precise visual marker to line up against the matching spike in the waveform.

Key Point. Once a rough offset is corrected, Otieno re-checks sync at more than one point across the clip — the start, the middle, and near the end — since two recordings made at slightly different times can drift apart over a long clip even after the opening lines match perfectly.
