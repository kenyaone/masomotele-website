Choosing Resolution and Frame Rate for Export

Source Resolution vs Export Resolution

When Otieno films a shop promo on his phone, the footage is captured at a source resolution — often 1080p, sometimes 4K if his phone and storage allow it. When he finishes editing in CapCut or Canva's Video editor and opens the export screen, he then chooses an export resolution: the resolution the final file is actually rendered at.

These two numbers do not have to match, but they should never let the export resolution claim more detail than the source actually has. If Otieno films at 1080p and sets the export screen to 4K, CapCut will happily render a 4K file — but it is only upscaled 1080p, not genuine 4K detail. The file gets bigger and takes longer to render without looking any sharper.

Frame Rate: 24fps, 30fps, 60fps

Frame rate is how many frames make up one second of video. 24fps gives a slightly more cinematic, filmic motion feel and is common for narrative-style edits. 30fps is the standard, safe choice for most social and event content — smooth without needing extra processing. 60fps captures noticeably smoother motion and is the frame rate you want if you plan to slow footage down afterwards for a slow-motion effect, since there are more frames to stretch across the slowed clip.

Otieno's phone camera lets him choose the capture frame rate before he even starts filming, and CapCut's export screen lets him choose the export frame rate separately — the two should match unless he has a specific creative reason (like slow motion) to do otherwise.

Matching Export Settings to the Source

The safest rule for Otieno is: export resolution and frame rate should match what was actually captured, unless the destination platform has a hard requirement that forces a change (some platforms cap resolution or frame rate on upload). Exporting lower than the source (downscaling) is sometimes reasonable to hit a platform's file-size limit, but exporting higher than the source (upscaling) almost never helps — it just makes the file heavier to upload and slower to process.

Key Point. Check what the footage was actually filmed at before setting export resolution and frame rate. Match them to the source by default; only deviate deliberately — for example, capturing at 60fps specifically to enable smooth slow motion, or downscaling resolution to meet a platform's upload limit.
