Basic DC Circuit Concepts

Voltage, Current, Resistance — the three pillars

Key Definitions

Direct Current (DC): Electric current that flows in one direction only. Source examples: batteries, solar cells, DC power supplies.
Voltage (V): The electrical pressure that drives current through a circuit. Measured in Volts (V). Symbol: V or E.
Current (I): The rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor. Measured in Amperes (A). Symbol: I.
Resistance (R): The opposition to the flow of electric current in a circuit. Measured in Ohms (Ω). Symbol: R.
Electric Circuit: A closed path through which electric current flows from a source, through components, and back to the source.

Circuit Symbols

Ohm's Law

The fundamental law of DC circuits

Statement of Ohm's Law

Ohm's Law: The current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage across it, provided temperature and other physical conditions remain constant.
SymbolQuantityUnitInstrument to Measure
VVoltage (EMF or potential difference)Volts (V)Voltmeter — connected in parallel
ICurrentAmperes (A)Ammeter — connected in series
RResistanceOhms (Ω)Ohmmeter / Multimeter

✏️ Worked Examples

Example 1: A resistor has a resistance of 10Ω. If a voltage of 20V is applied across it, what current flows?

I = V ÷ R = 20 ÷ 10 = 2 A

Example 2: A current of 3A flows through a 15Ω resistor. What is the voltage across it?

V = I × R = 3 × 15 = 45 V

Example 3: A 12V battery drives a current of 4A through a resistor. What is the resistance?

R = V ÷ I = 12 ÷ 4 = 3 Ω

Series Resistor Circuits

One path for current to flow

Properties of a Series Circuit

Series circuit: A circuit in which all components are connected end-to-end along a single path so the same current flows through all components.
PropertyFormulaExplanation
Total ResistanceRT = R1 + R2 + R3Resistances add up
CurrentI = VT ÷ RTSame current everywhere
VoltageVT = V1 + V2 + V3Voltages add up to supply
Individual VoltageV = I × ROhm's Law per resistor

✏️ Worked Example — Series Circuit

R1=4Ω, R2=6Ω, R3=2Ω connected in series to a 12V battery.

RT = 4+6+2 = 12Ω

I = 12÷12 = 1A (same through all)

V1 = 1×4 = 4V  |  V2 = 1×6 = 6V  |  V3 = 1×2 = 2V

Check: 4+6+2 = 12V ✓

Parallel Resistor Circuits

Multiple paths for current to flow

Properties of a Parallel Circuit

Parallel circuit: A circuit in which components are connected across the same two points (nodes), providing multiple paths for current to flow. The same voltage appears across all branches.
PropertyFormulaExplanation
Total Resistance1/RT = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3RT is less than smallest R
VoltageVT = V1 = V2 = V3Same voltage across all
Branch CurrentIn = V ÷ RnEach branch has own current
Total CurrentIT = I1 + I2 + I3Currents add at junction

✏️ Worked Example — Parallel Circuit

R1=6Ω, R2=12Ω, R3=4Ω in parallel with 12V supply.

I1=12/6=2A  |  I2=12/12=1A  |  I3=12/4=3A

IT=2+1+3=6A

RT=V/IT=12/6=2Ω (less than smallest R=4Ω ✓)

Kirchhoff's Laws

Analysing complex DC circuits

Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)

KCL: The total current entering a junction equals the total current leaving the junction. ΣIin = ΣIout

✏️ KCL Example

At a junction: I1 = 5A enters, I2 = 2A leaves. What is I3 (the other leaving current)?

5 = 2 + I3  →  I3 = 3A

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)

KVL: The sum of all voltages around any closed loop in a circuit equals zero. In other words, the total EMF equals the total voltage drops. ΣV = 0

✏️ KVL Example

A loop has a 12V battery and three resistors. V1=3V, V2=5V. Find V3.

12 = V1 + V2 + V3  →  12 = 3 + 5 + V3  →  V3 = 4V

Faults in DC Circuits

Identifying and troubleshooting problems

Common DC Circuit Faults

Open circuit: A break in the circuit path that prevents current from flowing. The circuit is incomplete — no current flows anywhere.
Short circuit: An unintended low-resistance path that bypasses part of the circuit, causing very high current to flow, which can damage components or start a fire.
High resistance fault: A connection that has developed resistance (e.g., loose terminal, dry solder joint) causing reduced current and voltage drop at that point.
Fault TypeSymptomCauseHow to Find It
Open CircuitNo current flows; circuit deadBroken wire, blown fuse, open switchVoltmeter — shows full supply voltage across open
Short CircuitHigh current; fuse blows; heatBare wires touching, faulty insulationAmmeter reads very high; ohmmeter shows 0Ω
Faulty ComponentIncorrect readingsBurnt/degraded resistorReplace component; test with ohmmeter
Dry/Loose JointIntermittent faultPoor solder, loose terminalVisual check; tap test; voltmeter

⚠️ Short Circuit Safety

A short circuit can cause fire, explosion of batteries, and severe burns. Always use fuses or circuit breakers to protect circuits. Never bypass protective devices.

✏️ Fault-Finding Procedure

1. Observe — What symptoms are present? (No light? Burning smell?)

2. Isolate — Switch off and disconnect the supply for safety.

3. Measure — Use voltmeter/ohmmeter to locate the fault.

4. Identify — Pinpoint the faulty component or connection.

5. Repair — Replace or fix the fault, then retest.

Practice Problems

Apply Ohm's Law and circuit analysis
Problem 1: Ohm's Law

A 24V battery is connected across a 8Ω resistor. Calculate: (a) the current, (b) the power dissipated.

📌 Solution

(a) I = V ÷ R = 24 ÷ 8 = 3 A

(b) P = V × I = 24 × 3 = 72 W  (or P = I²R = 9×8 = 72W)

Problem 2: Series Circuit

Three resistors R1=5Ω, R2=10Ω, R3=15Ω are connected in series to a 30V supply. Find: (a) total resistance, (b) current, (c) voltage across each resistor.

📌 Solution

(a) RT = 5+10+15 = 30 Ω

(b) I = 30÷30 = 1 A

(c) V1=1×5=5V | V2=1×10=10V | V3=1×15=15V

Check: 5+10+15 = 30V ✓

Problem 3: Parallel Circuit

Two resistors R1=8Ω and R2=24Ω are connected in parallel across an 8V supply. Find: (a) current in each branch, (b) total current, (c) total resistance.

📌 Solution

(a) I1=8÷8=1A | I2=8÷24=0.33A

(b) IT=1+0.33=1.33A

(c) RT=8÷1.33=6Ω (or: 1/RT=1/8+1/24=3/24+1/24=4/24 → RT=6Ω ✓)

Problem 4: Fault Identification

In a series circuit with three bulbs and a 12V battery, one bulb is removed and all bulbs go off. In a parallel circuit with three bulbs, one bulb is removed and the others stay on. Explain why.

📌 Answer

Series: There is only ONE path for current. Removing a bulb creates an open circuit — no current flows anywhere, so all bulbs go off.

Parallel: Each bulb has its own separate path. Removing one bulb only opens that one branch — current still flows through the other branches, so the remaining bulbs stay on.
