Magnetic Properties of Materials

Not all materials respond to magnets the same way

Classification of Materials

Ferromagnetic materials: Strongly attracted to magnets and can be permanently magnetised. Examples: iron, steel, nickel, cobalt.
Paramagnetic materials: Weakly attracted to magnets but cannot be permanently magnetised. Examples: aluminium, platinum, manganese.
Diamagnetic materials: Weakly repelled by magnets. Examples: copper, gold, silver, water, wood, plastic.
Non-magnetic materials: Show no significant response to magnetic fields. Examples: glass, rubber, most plastics.

Magnetic Domain Theory

Magnetic domain: A small region within a ferromagnetic material where all atomic magnetic moments are aligned in the same direction. In an unmagnetised material, domains point in random directions and cancel out. Magnetisation aligns them.

Magnetic Field Patterns

Visualising invisible forces

Key Definitions

Magnetic field: The region around a magnet where magnetic forces act on other magnetic materials or moving charges.
Magnetic field lines (flux lines): Imaginary lines that show the direction and strength of a magnetic field. They flow from North to South outside the magnet, and South to North inside. Lines never cross.
Magnetic flux (Φ): The total number of magnetic field lines passing through an area. Unit: Weber (Wb).
Magnetic flux density (B): The number of flux lines per unit area. Unit: Tesla (T). B = Φ/A

Field Patterns Around Different Magnet Shapes

Rules for Magnetic Field Lines

Magnetisation Methods

How to create a magnet from a magnetic material

Methods of Magnetisation

MethodProcedureResult
Single strokeStroke a magnet along the material in one direction only, repeatedly, lifting at the end of each strokeWeak to moderate magnetisation
Double stroke (divided)Two magnets stroked from centre outward simultaneously in opposite directionsStronger magnetisation than single stroke
Electrical methodPlace material inside a solenoid (coil) carrying DC current — most effective methodStrong, uniform magnetisation
Hammering in a fieldStrike material repeatedly while held in alignment with Earth's magnetic field or near a magnetWeak magnetisation

Temporary vs Permanent Magnets

Temporary magnet (soft iron): Easily magnetised but loses magnetism quickly when the magnetising force is removed. Used in electromagnets, transformers, relay cores.
Permanent magnet (hard steel / alloys): Retains magnetism for a long time after magnetisation. Used in motors, speakers, generators, compasses. Alloy examples: Alnico, Ferrite, Neodymium (NdFeB).

Demagnetisation

How to destroy a magnet's magnetism

Methods of Demagnetisation

Demagnetisation: The process of removing or destroying the magnetism of a magnet by disrupting the alignment of magnetic domains.
MethodHow It WorksEffectiveness
Heating (above Curie point)Heat disrupts domain alignment. Above the Curie temperature (~770°C for iron), domains become randomVery effective — permanent
Hammering / mechanical shockPhysical vibration disrupts domain alignmentModerate — partial demagnetisation
AC electrical methodPlace magnet in an AC solenoid and slowly withdraw — alternating field randomises domainsMost controlled and complete
Rough handling / droppingRepeated physical shocks gradually weaken magnetismSlow and partial

💡 Curie Temperature

The Curie temperature is the temperature above which a ferromagnetic material loses its permanent magnetism and becomes paramagnetic. For iron: ~770°C. For nickel: ~358°C. For cobalt: ~1115°C.

This is why magnets should not be stored near heat sources — elevated temperatures will gradually weaken them.

⚠️ Accidental Demagnetisation in the Workplace

Permanent magnets used in motors, generators, and instruments can be accidentally demagnetised by: placing them near strong AC fields, dropping them on hard surfaces, storing near heat sources, or storing like poles facing each other. Always handle and store magnets correctly.

Proper Magnet Storage

Magnetic keeper: A soft iron bar placed across the poles of a horseshoe magnet during storage. It completes the magnetic circuit, preventing flux leakage and maintaining magnetism.

📋 Storage Rules for Workplace Magnets

• Store bar magnets in pairs with unlike poles adjacent (N next to S) with iron keepers across the ends.

• Store horseshoe magnets with a soft iron keeper across both poles.

• Keep away from heat sources — never store above 50°C.

• Keep away from strong AC fields (transformers, motors).

• Avoid mechanical shocks — do not drop or bang magnets.

• Store away from electronic equipment — magnetic fields can corrupt data on hard drives, credit cards, etc.

• Keep like poles facing away from each other — never store N to N or S to S.

💡 Earth's Magnetic Field

The Earth itself acts like a giant bar magnet with its magnetic South pole near the geographic North Pole (that's why a compass needle's North pole points toward geographic North). The Earth's field has a flux density of about 25–65 µT at the surface. This field protects us from harmful solar wind particles.

Activity C: Magnetisation & Demagnetisation

A steel screwdriver has been accidentally magnetised and keeps picking up metal filings. Describe TWO methods to demagnetise it.

📌 Model Answer

Method 1 — AC Electrical: Place the screwdriver inside a solenoid connected to AC mains. Slowly withdraw the screwdriver while the AC is on. The alternating field randomises domain alignment, removing magnetism.

Method 2 — Heating: Heat the screwdriver above its Curie temperature (for steel ≈770°C). The heat disrupts domain alignment. Allow to cool away from any magnetic field.

Activity D: Magnet Storage

A technician leaves two bar magnets with North poles facing each other in a drawer near a transformer. List THREE mistakes and explain why each is harmful to the magnets.

📌 Answer

1. Like poles facing — repulsion between domains causes demagnetisation over time.

2. Near a transformer — transformer produces AC magnetic fields that randomise domains, weakening the magnets.

3. No keepers — without soft iron keepers, flux leaks into the air, causing the magnets to gradually lose their magnetism (self-demagnetisation).
