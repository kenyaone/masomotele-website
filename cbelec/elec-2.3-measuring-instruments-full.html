The Moving Coil Instrument

How a galvanometer works

Principle of Operation

Moving Coil Instrument (Galvanometer): A sensitive instrument that detects and measures small electric currents. It works on the principle that a current-carrying coil placed in a magnetic field experiences a turning force (torque).
D'Arsonval Movement: The most common moving coil mechanism. A rectangular coil wound on an aluminium former is suspended between the poles of a permanent magnet. Current through the coil creates a force that rotates it against a restoring spring.

Construction of the Moving Coil Instrument

ComponentRole
Permanent magnetProvides the fixed magnetic field in which the coil rotates
Soft iron cylinderConcentrates flux and ensures uniform (radial) field for linear scale
Moving coilCarries the current being measured; experiences torque F = BIlN
PointerIndicates reading on the calibrated scale
Restoring spring (hairspring)Returns pointer to zero; provides counter-torque proportional to deflection
Aluminium formerProvides eddy current damping — prevents pointer oscillation

Converting Galvanometer to Ammeter

Using a shunt resistor to extend current range

The Shunt Resistor

Shunt (Rsh): A low-value resistor connected in parallel with the galvanometer coil to bypass most of the current and allow the instrument to measure larger currents.
Full Scale Deflection (FSD) current (Ig): The maximum current that can flow through the galvanometer coil without damage. Typically in the range of microamperes to milliamperes.

Shunt Formula Derivation

📐 Shunt Formula

Since shunt and galvanometer are in parallel: Voltage across both must be equal

Ig × Rg = (I − Ig) × Rsh

Therefore: Rsh = (Ig × Rg) / (I − Ig)

Where: Ig = FSD current of galvanometer, Rg = galvanometer coil resistance, I = desired full-scale current of ammeter

✏️ Worked Example

A galvanometer has Ig = 5mA and Rg = 20Ω. Convert to an ammeter reading 0–2A.

Rsh = (Ig × Rg) / (I − Ig) = (0.005 × 20) / (2 − 0.005)

Rsh = 0.1 / 1.995 = 0.0501Ω ≈ 50mΩ

Converting Galvanometer to Voltmeter

Using a multiplier resistor to extend voltage range

The Multiplier Resistor

Multiplier (Rm): A high-value resistor connected in series with the galvanometer coil to limit current and allow the instrument to measure higher voltages. The voltmeter must have very high resistance to avoid loading the circuit.

📐 Multiplier Formula

Total voltage: V = Ig × (Rg + Rm)

Therefore: Rm = (V / Ig) − Rg

Where: V = desired full-scale voltage, Ig = FSD current, Rg = galvanometer resistance

✏️ Worked Example

A galvanometer has Ig = 1mA and Rg = 50Ω. Convert to a voltmeter reading 0–10V.

Rm = (V / Ig) − Rg = (10 / 0.001) − 50 = 10,000 − 50 = 9,950Ω ≈ 10kΩ

Ohmmeter & Multimeter

Measuring resistance and combining all functions

The Ohmmeter

Ohmmeter: An instrument that measures electrical resistance. It contains its own internal battery and works by measuring the current that flows through the unknown resistance.

📋 How an Ohmmeter Works

1. Internal battery provides a fixed EMF.

2. When probes are shorted together (R=0) → maximum current flows → pointer reads 0Ω (full deflection, right side).

3. When probes are open (R=∞) → no current → pointer at rest = ∞Ω (left side).

4. Unknown R connected → current flows inversely proportional to R → pointer deflects to show resistance.

Note: Ohmmeter scale reads RIGHT to LEFT (opposite to voltage/current). Always zero-adjust before use.

⚠️ Critical Ohmmeter Safety Rule

NEVER use an ohmmeter on a live circuit. The internal battery provides its own voltage — external voltage will give false readings and can permanently damage the instrument. Always isolate the circuit and discharge capacitors first.

The Multimeter

Multimeter: A single instrument that combines voltmeter, ammeter, and ohmmeter functions. Available as analogue (moving coil) or digital (DMM — Digital Multimeter).
TypeAdvantagesDisadvantages
Analogue multimeterShows trends; works without battery for V/I; cheapParallax error; scale reading difficulty; fragile
Digital multimeter (DMM)High accuracy; auto-ranging; easy to read; robustNeeds battery; slower for trend reading; more expensive

Calibration of Measuring Instruments

Ensuring accuracy before measurement

What is Calibration?

Calibration: The process of configuring or adjusting a measuring instrument so that its readings correspond to a known standard. An uncalibrated instrument gives inaccurate or unreliable readings.
Calibration TypeWhat It DoesHow It's Done
Zero calibrationSets pointer to exactly zero before measurementAdjust zero screw on instrument face with no input connected
Ohmmeter zero-adjustCorrects for battery aging in ohmmeterShort probes together and turn ZERO ADJ knob until pointer reads 0Ω
Range calibrationVerifies accuracy at full scale and mid-scaleApply known voltage/current from reference standard; adjust if needed
Fine-tuning / span adjustCorrects gain error across the full scaleApply known full-scale input; adjust span potentiometer

Sources of Error in Measurement

Error TypeCauseHow to Avoid
Parallax errorReading scale at an angle (not directly in front)View pointer perpendicular to scale; use mirror scale
Loading errorAmmeter too high R; voltmeter too low R changes circuitUse instrument with appropriate resistance for the circuit
Zero errorPointer not at zero before measurementZero-adjust before every measurement session
Range errorWrong range selected — pointer barely deflects or goes off-scaleAlways start on highest range; reduce to appropriate range
Temperature errorInstrument resistance changes with temperatureAllow warm-up time; use at rated temperature

📋 Measurement Best Practice

1. Select correct instrument — right quantity, right range.

2. Zero-adjust before each use.

3. Start on highest range, reduce until pointer is in upper half of scale.

4. Read directly in front — avoid parallax.

5. Record reading with correct unit and range setting.

6. Store properly — cover, protect from magnetic fields and mechanical shock.

⚠️ Never Connect Ammeter in Parallel

An ammeter has very low resistance. Connecting it in parallel across a voltage source creates a near short-circuit — very high current destroys the shunt and coil immediately. Always connect ammeters in series with the load.

Practice Problems

Shunts, multipliers and instrument use
Problem 1: Shunt Calculation

A galvanometer with FSD current of 10mA and coil resistance of 15Ω is to be converted to an ammeter with a range of 0–5A. Calculate the required shunt resistance.

📌 Solution

Rsh = (Ig × Rg) / (I − Ig)

Rsh = (0.01 × 15) / (5 − 0.01) = 0.15 / 4.99 = 0.03Ω ≈ 30mΩ

Problem 2: Multiplier Calculation

A galvanometer has FSD current of 2mA and coil resistance of 100Ω. Calculate the multiplier needed for: (a) 50V range, (b) 250V range.

📌 Solution

(a) Rm = (50 / 0.002) − 100 = 25,000 − 100 = 24,900Ω ≈ 24.9kΩ

(b) Rm = (250 / 0.002) − 100 = 125,000 − 100 = 124,900Ω ≈ 124.9kΩ

Problem 3: Reading a Scale

An analogue voltmeter has 50 scale divisions and is set to the 150V range. The pointer rests at division 35. What is the voltage reading?

📌 Solution

Scale factor = Full Scale Value / Total divisions = 150 / 50 = 3 V per division

Reading = 35 × 3 = 105V

Problem 4: Instrument Selection

A technician needs to measure the current through a 60W, 240V light bulb when it is working normally. Select the appropriate instrument, range, and connection method.

📌 Answer

Expected current: I = P/V = 60/240 = 0.25A

Instrument: Ammeter or DMM set to AC current

Range: 1A AC range (next range above 0.25A)

Connection: In series with the bulb — break the circuit and insert the ammeter in series.
