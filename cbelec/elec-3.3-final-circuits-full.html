What Are Final Circuits?

The last stage of the distribution system

Definition & Purpose

Final circuit: The wiring from the last overcurrent protective device (MCB/fuse) in the consumer control unit to the current-using equipment (lamps, sockets, cooker, etc.). It is the part of the installation that the consumer directly uses.
Current-using equipment: Any device that converts electrical energy to another form — lamps (light), heaters (heat), motors (motion), bells (sound).

💡 Why Separate Circuits?

Each type of load gets its own circuit so that: (1) a fault on one circuit doesn't affect others, (2) each circuit is protected by the correct size MCB, (3) cables are sized correctly for the current, and (4) heavy loads like cookers don't overload the lighting wiring.

Electrical Wiring Diagrams & Symbols

The language of electrical installation

Types of Wiring Diagrams

Layout diagram: Shows the physical positions of components on a floor plan of the building. Used for planning cable routes and positioning of switches and sockets.
Circuit diagram (schematic): Shows the electrical connections between components using standard symbols. Does not show physical positions. Used for understanding how the circuit works.
Wiring diagram: Shows the actual connections as they would be made in practice, including which terminal each wire connects to.

Common Wiring Diagram Symbols

Lighting Circuits

One-way, two-way and intermediate switching

One-Way (Simple) Switch Circuit

One-way switch: Controls a lamp from ONE point only. Has two terminals — Common (C) and L1. When switched on, current flows from Live through the switch to the lamp and back via Neutral.

Two-Way Switch Circuit

Two-way switching: Controls a lamp from TWO points (e.g. top and bottom of stairs). Each switch has three terminals: Common (C), L1, L2. The two switches are interconnected by two switch wires (strapper wires).

📋 Two-Way Switch Connections

• Switch 1: C connected to Live supply; L1 and L2 connected to strapper wires

• Switch 2: C connected to lamp; L1 and L2 connected to other ends of strapper wires

• Lamp: one terminal to Switch 2 Common; other to Neutral

• The lamp can now be switched ON or OFF from either switch independently.

Socket Outlet & Cooker Circuits

Power circuits for appliances

Socket Outlet (Ring Main) Circuit

Ring main circuit: A circuit where the cable runs from the MCB, visits each socket in turn, and returns to the same MCB. This provides two paths for current to each socket, reducing voltage drop and allowing higher total loading.

Cooker Circuit

Cooker circuit: A dedicated final circuit for an electric cooker. Uses large cable (6mm²–10mm²) protected by a 32A–45A MCB. A cooker control unit (switch + socket) is installed near the cooker.

📋 Cooker Circuit Requirements

• Dedicated circuit — cooker must have its own MCB, not shared with other loads.

• Cable size: minimum 6mm² twin + earth for domestic cooker up to 45A.

• Cooker control unit: a double-pole switch (to isolate cooker for maintenance) with an optional 13A socket for a kettle.

• Heat-resistant cable between control unit and cooker if cable runs near heat source.

• MCB rating: typically 32A for small cooker, 45A for full range cooker.

Bell Circuit

Bell circuit: A low-voltage circuit (typically 6V–12V AC from a bell transformer) operating a doorbell, chime, or alarm bell. Uses thin bell wire (0.5mm²). The transformer steps down 240V to safe low voltage.

📋 Bell Circuit Components

1. Bell transformer — 240V primary → 6V or 12V secondary (plugs into 13A socket or fixed to distribution board)

2. Push button — momentary contact switch at the door

3. Bell/chime — sounds when button is pressed

4. Bell wire — 0.5mm² twin flex connecting all components

Installation Procedure & Testing

From first fix to final test

Installation Sequence

📋 Step-by-Step Installation Procedure

First Fix (before plastering):

1. Plan — Mark positions of switches, sockets, and luminaires on walls and ceiling.

2. Chase walls — Cut channels in walls for recessed conduit (use grinder/chisel).

3. Fix conduit/trunking — Install conduit and back boxes in chased channels.

4. Pull cables — Draw cables through conduit from DB to each outlet position.

5. Leave tails — Leave 200–300mm of cable at each outlet for connections.

Second Fix (after plastering/painting):

6. Connect outlets — Wire switches, sockets, ceiling roses at each point.

7. Connect DB — Connect circuit cables to MCBs in the distribution board.

8. Label circuits — Mark each MCB with the circuit it protects.

⚠️ Safety During Installation

• Always isolate and lock off the supply before working on any circuit.
• Test with a voltage tester before touching any conductor — even after isolating.
• Wear appropriate PPE — insulated gloves, safety boots, safety goggles when drilling.
• Never work on live circuits without specific authorisation and safe isolation procedures.

Problem 3: Fault Finding

In a ring main circuit, one socket has no power but all others on the ring work. The MCB has not tripped. What is the likely fault and how would you find it?

📌 Answer

Likely fault: Open circuit at the faulty socket — a loose connection at one of the terminals (Live, Neutral, or Earth) inside the socket back box, or a broken conductor in the spur/loop to that socket.

Finding it:

1. Isolate circuit (switch off MCB, lock out).

2. Remove socket front plate — inspect terminals for loose connections, signs of burning.

3. Use ohmmeter to test continuity of each conductor to the socket terminals.

4. Re-tighten or re-make connection, retest.
