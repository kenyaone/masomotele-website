Conductors, Insulators & Semiconductors

Classification of materials by electrical conductivity

The Three Classes of Materials

Conductor: A material that allows electric current to flow easily. Has many free electrons in its outer shell (valence band overlaps conduction band). Examples: copper, silver, aluminium, gold.
Insulator: A material that strongly resists current flow. Valence electrons are tightly bound — a large energy gap (bandgap >5eV) prevents electrons reaching the conduction band. Examples: rubber, glass, PVC, wood, ceramic.
Semiconductor: A material with conductivity between a conductor and an insulator. Has a small energy gap (~1eV) that electrons can cross with thermal energy or doping. Examples: silicon (Si), germanium (Ge), gallium arsenide (GaAs).

Why Semiconductors are Special

Atomic Structure of Silicon

Understanding the building block of electronics

Silicon — The Most Important Semiconductor

Silicon (Si): Atomic number 14. Has 4 valence electrons in its outer shell. Most widely used semiconductor — forms the basis of 95%+ of all electronic devices including transistors, integrated circuits, and solar cells.
Germanium (Ge): Atomic number 32. Also has 4 valence electrons. Used in early transistors; now mainly used in high-speed and infrared devices.

Covalent Bonding in Silicon

Covalent bond: A chemical bond formed when two atoms share a pair of electrons. In silicon crystal, each atom shares its 4 valence electrons with 4 neighbouring atoms, forming a stable lattice with 8 electrons in each outer shell (octet rule).

💡 Silicon Crystal Structure

In a pure silicon crystal: each Si atom is bonded to 4 others by covalent bonds. All valence electrons are shared — there are no free electrons at absolute zero temperature.

At room temperature: thermal energy breaks a few bonds, freeing electrons and creating holes (positive vacancies). This gives pure silicon its limited conductivity.

Intrinsic Semiconductors

Pure semiconductor behaviour

What is an Intrinsic Semiconductor?

Intrinsic semiconductor: A pure semiconductor material with no impurity atoms added. Silicon or germanium in its chemically pure form. Conductivity depends entirely on temperature — the higher the temperature, the more electron-hole pairs are generated.
Electron-hole pair: When thermal energy breaks a covalent bond, a free electron and a hole (positive vacancy) are created simultaneously. These are charge carriers — electrons move toward the positive terminal, holes toward the negative terminal.
Hole: The absence of an electron in a covalent bond. It behaves like a positive charge carrier. Holes move in the opposite direction to electrons when voltage is applied.

Conduction in Intrinsic Silicon

TemperatureElectron-Hole PairsConductivity
Absolute zero (0K / −273°C)Zero — all bonds intactZero — perfect insulator
Room temperature (~300K)Few — limited thermal energyVery low — poor conductor
Elevated temperatureMany — more bonds brokenIncreases — more carriers

💡 Key Intrinsic Properties

• Equal numbers of electrons and holes: n = p = ni (intrinsic carrier concentration)

• For silicon at 300K: ni ≈ 1.5 × 10¹⁰ carriers/cm³ (very few compared to copper's ~8.5 × 10²² e⁻/cm³)

• Pure silicon is too resistive for most electronic applications → doping is needed

Doping — N-Type Semiconductor

Adding donor impurities to increase free electrons

What is Doping?

Doping: The deliberate addition of tiny amounts of impurity atoms (dopants) into a pure semiconductor to dramatically increase its conductivity and control the type of charge carriers. Typical doping level: 1 impurity atom per 10⁶–10⁸ silicon atoms.

N-Type Semiconductor

N-type semiconductor: Formed by adding a pentavalent (5 valence electrons) donor impurity to silicon. The extra electron from the dopant becomes a free electron. Majority carriers = electrons; minority carriers = holes.
Donor impurities (pentavalent): Phosphorus (P), Arsenic (As), Antimony (Sb). Each donor atom donates one free electron to the crystal lattice.

Doping — P-Type Semiconductor

Adding acceptor impurities to increase holes

P-Type Semiconductor

P-type semiconductor: Formed by adding a trivalent (3 valence electrons) acceptor impurity to silicon. A hole (missing electron) is created at each dopant atom. Majority carriers = holes; minority carriers = electrons.
Acceptor impurities (trivalent): Boron (B), Aluminium (Al), Gallium (Ga), Indium (In). Each acceptor atom accepts an electron from a neighbouring bond, creating a hole.

The PN Junction

The heart of all semiconductor devices

Formation of the PN Junction

PN junction: The boundary formed when P-type and N-type semiconductor materials are joined together. This junction is the fundamental building block of diodes, transistors, solar cells, and LEDs.

The Depletion Layer

Depletion layer (depletion region): A thin region at the PN junction that is depleted of free charge carriers. It forms automatically when P and N materials are joined, as electrons from the N-side diffuse across and recombine with holes on the P-side.
FeatureDetail
Built-in potential (barrier voltage)~0.7V for silicon; ~0.3V for germanium
Width of depletion layerTypically 0.5–1 µm at equilibrium
Electric field in depletion layerPoints from N to P (opposes further diffusion)
Charges in depletion layerPositive ions on N-side; negative ions on P-side

Activity B: Doping Questions
  1. Silicon is doped with arsenic. What type of semiconductor is formed and what are the majority carriers?
    → N-type. Arsenic has 5 valence electrons (pentavalent). Majority carriers = electrons.
  2. Silicon is doped with boron. What type and majority carriers?
    → P-type. Boron has 3 valence electrons (trivalent). Majority carriers = holes.
  3. Why is an N-type semiconductor still electrically neutral even though it has extra electrons?
    → The donor atoms (e.g. phosphorus) each contribute one free electron but also contribute one positive ion (the atom itself). The positive ion is fixed in the lattice, balancing the negative free electron. Total charge = zero.
Activity C: PN Junction

Explain what happens when P-type and N-type silicon are joined to form a PN junction. Include: diffusion, depletion layer, built-in potential.

📌 Model Answer

When P and N materials are joined: electrons from the N-side diffuse across the junction toward the P-side (high concentration → low concentration). They recombine with holes on the P-side.

This creates a depletion layer — a region devoid of free carriers. Positive ions remain on the N-side; negative ions on the P-side.

The ion charges create an electric field and a built-in potential (~0.7V for silicon) that opposes further diffusion. Equilibrium is reached when the diffusion force equals the electric field force.
