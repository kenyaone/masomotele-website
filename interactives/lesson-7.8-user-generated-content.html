3. Audit 6 UGC practices

Mark each practice good or bad. Auto-scored.

A. Sarah screenshots a customer's Instagram story and reposts it to the Karibu Kitchen feed without messaging first.
Bad — no consent. DPA violation and a fast route to being blocked.
B. Sarah DMs the customer: "Hi Wanjiku, may we share your lunch photo on our Instagram, with credit to you?" She waits for a yes before posting.
Good — explicit consent, purpose stated, credit promised. Textbook DPA.
C. Sarah edits the customer's original caption to "This is the BEST lunch in Eldoret" before reposting.
Bad — putting words in the customer's mouth is misrepresentation. Never edit their wording.
D. Sarah tags the original creator's handle in the repost caption and keeps their watermark visible.
Good — credit builds trust. Customers keep sending photos when they feel seen.
E. A customer asks Sarah to take down their photo. Sarah replies "It's already up, sorry" and leaves it live.
Bad — the DPA gives every data subject the right to withdraw. Remove within 24 hours or face a complaint.
F. Sarah runs a monthly draw: any customer who tags #KaribuKitchen and consents to a repost wins a free lunch. Terms are posted in her Instagram bio.
Good — transparent terms, real incentive, opt-in via hashtag. This is exactly Sarah's playbook.

4. Sarah's monthly UGC playbook

  1. Week 1 — Print 100 "share your plate" cards. One in every delivery bag.
  2. Every day — Reply to every tag or story mention within 4 hours.
  3. Every Friday — Post "Customer of the Week" Story. Credit the creator.
  4. Month-end — Draw one lunch giveaway from the month's UGC contributors.
  5. Ongoing — Keep a UGC folder with every consent screenshot. Backup to Drive weekly.
Cost: ~KES 2,000/month in giveaway lunches. Value: customer content flowing daily. UGC beats any paid ad Sarah could run at that budget.
